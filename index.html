Ziaur Rahman played an important role in the political development of Bangladesh during the political and military problems after 1975. On 7 November 1975, he became Chief of Army Staff again. Later, he became Deputy Chief Martial Law Administrator and Chief Martial Law Administrator (CMLA).

He slowly tried to bring political and constitutional order. He allowed some political activities, made changes to the Constitution, held elections, and formed a political party.

On 28 July 1976, Zia introduced the Political Parties Regulation (PPR). It partly removed the ban on political activities. Political parties could do indoor political activities under government rules. This gave people more chances to take part in politics and helped develop multi-party politics.

On 23 April 1977, Zia made important changes to the Constitution. “Bismillah-Ar-Rahman-Ar-Rahim” was added to the Constitution. Secularism was replaced by “absolute trust and faith in the Almighty Allah.” Bengali Nationalism was also replaced with Bangladeshi Nationalism.

On 30 April 1977, he introduced the 19-Point Programme. It included food self-sufficiency, literacy, women's dignity, and decentralization.

Zia also held several elections. Union Parishad elections were held in January 1977. A referendum was held on 30 May 1977. Municipal elections were held in August–September 1977. On 3 June 1978, Zia won the presidential election with about 76.7% of the vote.

On 1 September 1978, he formed the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) and became its chairman. In the February 1979 parliamentary election, BNP won 207 out of 300 directly elected seats. After Parliament started working, martial law was formally ended.

Therefore, Ziaur Rahman's main political roles were opening political activities, developing multi-party politics, making constitutional changes, forming BNP, and holding elections. His period showed a gradual move from military rule toward an elected government.

The movement for democracy in post-independent Bangladesh developed through several political struggles. After independence in 1971, Bangladesh started its journey as a parliamentary democracy. However, political instability, military intervention, one-party rule, and authoritarian government created serious problems for democratic development. Different political parties, students, workers, professionals, and ordinary citizens participated in movements to restore democratic government.

On 25 January 1975, the Fourth Amendment abolished the parliamentary system and multi-party democracy. All political parties were dissolved and BAKSAL (Bangladesh Krishak Sramik Awami League) became the only legally recognized political party. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman became President with extensive executive powers. These changes reduced political pluralism and created criticism about the direction of democracy.

After this period, Bangladesh experienced military-backed governments. Ziaur Rahman gradually opened political activities and allowed political parties to participate again. The Political Parties Regulation of 28 July 1976 partially lifted the ban on political activities. BNP was formed on 1 September 1978 with Ziaur Rahman as its chairman. The parliamentary election of February 1979 also became an important step in returning to constitutional politics.

However, after the assassination of Ziaur Rahman, Bangladesh again entered a period of military rule. Hussain Muhammad Ershad came to power through a military coup on 24 March 1982. Political activities were restricted and democratic institutions were weakened. The demand for democracy became an important issue among political parties and citizens.

The democratic movement became more organized during the 1980s. One was a 15-party alliance led by Sheikh Hasina's Awami League, and the other was a 7-party alliance led by Khaleda Zia's BNP. These political groups demanded an end to martial law and the resignation of Ershad. On 6 September 1983, the two blocs presented a joint five-point charter demanding an end to martial law and Ershad's resignation.

Students also played a very important role in the democratic movement. On 14 February 1983, police fired on a student procession. This increased opposition to the military government. Students continued to organize demonstrations and protests against authoritarian rule.

The movement became stronger in 1987. A 54-hour hartal was organized, and opposition groups carried out demonstrations and blockades. On 28 October 1987, Sheikh Hasina and Khaleda Zia held a joint meeting to organize the Dhaka Aborodh. Police opened fire at Gulistan, and several people were killed.

One of the most important symbols of the democratic movement was Noor Hossain. On 10 November 1987, Noor Hossain, a young activist, was shot dead during the anti-Ershad movement. His body carried the message “Down with Autocracy, Let Democracy Live.” His death became a powerful symbol of the struggle for democracy.

In response to the growing opposition, Ershad declared a nationwide state of emergency on 27 November 1987. The opposition continued to demand democratic government. The 1988 election was boycotted by the Awami League, BNP, and Jamaat.

The democratic movement reached its final stage in 1990. The 8-party, 7-party, and 5-party alliances united again against Ershad. Students, workers, professionals, and civil society became involved in the movement. Dr. Shamsul Alam Khan Milon was killed by police at Dhaka University on 27 November 1990. His death further strengthened the movement.

By this time, Ershad was losing support. Facing an unstoppable mass movement and losing military backing, Hussain Muhammad Ershad resigned. His resignation ended about nine years of military-backed rule from 1982 to 1990.

After Ershad's resignation, Justice Shahabuddin Ahmed became Vice-President and then Acting President. On 6 December 1990, he became Acting President and headed a neutral caretaker government. This caretaker government prepared the country for a national election.

The next important step was the 27 February 1991 parliamentary election. The BNP won 140 seats, the Awami League won 88, the Jatiya Party won 35, and Jamaat-e-Islami won 18 seats. On 20 March 1991, Khaleda Zia became Prime Minister and became Bangladesh's first female Prime Minister.

The restoration of parliamentary democracy was formally completed through the 12th Constitutional Amendment in August 1991. This amendment changed Bangladesh from a presidential system back to a parliamentary system. Therefore, the democratic movement of the 1980s and 1990 played a major role in restoring parliamentary democracy in Bangladesh.

Multiparty democracy in Bangladesh developed through different political periods after independence. The 1972 Constitution established democracy as one of the four fundamental principles of the state. However, in 1975, the Fourth Constitutional Amendment introduced a presidential system and established BAKSAL as a one-party state, which ended the multiparty political system at that time.

During the period of Ziaur Rahman (1975–1981), multiparty politics was restored and the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) was founded. Later, during H. M. Ershad's rule (1982–1990), Bangladesh came under military-backed rule. Ershad founded the Jatiya Party on 1 January 1986 and held elections under military rule.

The 1986 election was contested by the Awami League, while the BNP-led 7-party alliance boycotted it. The Jatiya Party won amid allegations of election rigging.

The movement for democracy became stronger during the 1980s. Two major political blocs were formed: the 15-party alliance led by Sheikh Hasina's Awami League and the 7-party alliance led by Khaleda Zia's BNP.

On 6 September 1983, the opposition alliances issued a joint 5-point charter, demanding an end to martial law and the resignation of Ershad. In 1987, mass protests and hartals increased. Noor Hossain was killed on 10 November 1987, becoming an important symbol of the democratic movement.

On 27 November 1987, Ershad declared a nationwide state of emergency. The 1988 election was boycotted by the Awami League, BNP and Jamaat, while the Jatiya Party and its allies contested the election. The election was widely dismissed as "vote dacoity."

In 1990, the major opposition alliances united again against Ershad. The mass uprising eventually forced Ershad to resign on 6 December 1990, ending his rule.

After the fall of Ershad, Bangladesh returned to parliamentary democracy. The 1991 election was held after the democratic transition, and the Twelfth Constitutional Amendment of 1991 restored the parliamentary system of government. Later, the Thirteenth Constitutional Amendment of 1996 introduced the Non-Party Caretaker Government system.

Thus, the political history of Bangladesh shows repeated changes between democratic, one-party, military-backed and multiparty political systems.

Zia gradually used elections to establish political legitimacy. In January 1977, Union Parishad elections were held, with 4,352 councils going to the polls. A referendum was held on 30 May 1977 on the question of confidence in Zia. Municipal elections were also held in August–September 1977.

On 3 June 1978, Zia won the presidential election with approximately 76.7% of the vote.

Another major development was the formation of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) on 1 September 1978, with Ziaur Rahman as its chairman. The formation of BNP created a new major political force in Bangladesh.

In the February 1979 parliamentary election, BNP won 207 of the 300 directly elected seats, achieving a commanding majority. After the elected Parliament was seated, martial law was formally withdrawn, marking an important step toward constitutional political government.

Poverty means a condition in which people are unable to meet their basic needs such as food, clothing, shelter, education and medical treatment. It is one of the major social and economic problems of Bangladesh.

There are several causes of poverty in Bangladesh. The major causes include an underdeveloped economy and lack of resources, shortage of employment opportunities, illiteracy and lack of vocational training, rapid population growth, inefficient government measures and the lack of effective poverty alleviation programmes.

Another important cause is the vicious cycle of low saving, low investment and low income, which makes it difficult for poor people to improve their economic condition.

Poverty has several negative effects on society. It creates a large number of unskilled workers, which can reduce the competitiveness of industrial products in both domestic and international markets.

Poor people are also deprived of human dignity and often live in unsatisfactory conditions, creating social and health problems. Poverty creates differences between social classes and may cause discrimination in the enjoyment of civic rights.

Increasing poverty can also create socio-political unrest and become a threat to peace and stability. Furthermore, unemployment is one of the major factors responsible for poverty, while low income and saving reduce opportunities for investment and job creation.

The Government of Bangladesh has taken different measures to reduce poverty. These include the old-age allowance programme, widow and distressed women allowance programme, special micro-credit programmes for acid-burnt and physically disabled people, and training and self-employment programmes for insolvent freedom fighters and their wards.

Other measures include the Grihayan Tahbil, Karmasangsthan Bank, ABASHAN Project, rural infrastructure development, urban poverty reduction programmes, Palli Daridrya Bimochan Foundation (PDBF), Bangladesh Academy for Rural Development (BARD) and different micro-credit programmes.

Corruption is generally associated with bribery and taking undue benefits in return for doing a job. According to the UNO Dictionary of Social Science, corruption in public life means using public power for private profit, preference or prestige, or for the benefit of a group or clan in a way that violates law or high moral standards.

The Bangladesh Anti-Corruption Act considers corruption in terms of misconduct such as illegality, inefficiency, irregularity, lying, unethical behaviour and bribery.

There are several causes of corruption in Bangladesh. Unlimited greed is one of the major causes. Opportunities for corruption can also arise from biased recruitment, weak enforcement of laws, lack of monitoring and inactive anti-corruption authorities.

Low salaries and facilities may encourage corruption when income does not match the cost of living. Loopholes in laws and regulations can make it easier for corrupt activities to continue. Poor supervision and negligence at the supervisory level are also important causes.

Other factors include corrupt culture, political undue intervention, and the influence of greedy family members, friends and colleagues.

Several measures can be taken to control corruption. These include making the Anti-Corruption Commission independent and strong, identifying loopholes and taking preventive measures, taking departmental action against corrupt officials, regularly checking the accounts of public service holders, and publishing year-wise lists of corrupt persons.

Providing adequate salaries and facilities to public-sector employees, giving incentives to honest officials, using mobile courts, amending laws and increasing public awareness are also important measures.

Therefore, poverty and corruption are major problems that affect the economic and social development of Bangladesh. Reducing poverty requires employment, education, investment and effective poverty-alleviation programmes, while controlling corruption requires strong institutions, effective laws, proper supervision and public awareness.

An economic system is the system of rules, regulations, and conditions through which the economic activities of a country are organized and conducted. Every society needs an economic system because resources are limited while human wants are unlimited.

An economic system helps determine how resources will be used, what will be produced, how goods will be produced, and how goods and services will be distributed.

There are three major types of economic systems. These are the capitalistic economic system, the socialistic economic system, and the mixed economic system.

Capitalistic Economic System

In this system, the factors of production are mainly privately owned. Individuals and private organizations can own land, factories, businesses, and other property.

Production, exchange, and consumption are mainly guided by individual initiative and the desire to earn profit.

The government normally has a comparatively limited role in business and trade. Private individuals and businesses take most economic decisions. Prices are mainly determined through the interaction of demand and supply.

Another important characteristic of capitalism is consumer freedom. Consumers can choose the goods and services they want to purchase. Producers compete with one another to attract customers.

The profit motive is also an important characteristic of capitalism. Individuals and businesses invest money because they expect to earn profit.

Socialistic Economic System

In socialism, the state has major control over the factors of production. Land, factories, minerals, and other important forms of wealth are mainly controlled by the state or public sector.

Economic decisions are made through central planning. A central planning authority decides what should be produced, how much should be produced, and how production should take place.

The purpose is to organize production according to social needs rather than mainly according to individual profit.

Individual initiative in industry, trade, and commerce is more limited in a socialist system. Consumer choice can also be more limited because the state plays an important role in deciding what goods should be produced.

Mixed Economic System

A mixed system combines elements of capitalism and socialism. Both the private sector and the government sector take part in economic activities.

Individuals can own property and businesses, while the government also owns or controls some important sectors.

In a mixed economy, the market mechanism continues to operate. Prices are generally influenced by demand and supply. However, the government may regulate prices when necessary, especially for important or essential goods.

According to the presentation, Bangladesh follows a mixed economic system today.

In conclusion, there are three major economic systems: capitalism, socialism, and mixed economy. Capitalism emphasizes private ownership, competition, consumer choice, market prices, and profit. Socialism emphasizes state ownership, central planning, and production according to social needs. The mixed system combines private ownership and market activities with government ownership, regulation, and intervention.

Countries are different from one another in their level of economic development. They differ in per-capita income, standard of living, agriculture, industry, trade, technology, education, employment, capital, and infrastructure.

Characteristics of Underdeveloped or Least Developed Countries

A Least Developed Country (LDC) is a country where a high level of economic development has not yet been achieved. Such countries generally have low per-capita income and a low standard of living.

One important characteristic is low per-capita income. When per-capita income is low, people generally have fewer economic resources available for food, housing, education, health, and other needs.

Another characteristic is a low standard of living. Low income can affect people's ability to obtain adequate goods and services.

A further characteristic is over-dependence on agriculture. In many less developed economies, agriculture has a very important role.

Another characteristic is underdeveloped industry. When industrial development is weak, an economy may have fewer opportunities for manufacturing, investment, employment, and technological development.

Shortage of capital is another important characteristic. When there is little saving and investment, productive capacity may remain low.

Another major problem is unemployment. Less developed countries may have widespread unemployment because the economy cannot create enough productive employment opportunities.

Lack of technical knowledge is another characteristic. Modern economic development requires knowledge and skills.

Poor transport and communication systems can also restrict development. High illiteracy and a high dependence on foreign aid may also be associated with LDC conditions.

Developed countries have achieved a much higher level of economic development. They generally have high per-capita income and a high standard of living.

One major characteristic of developed countries is extensive industrialization. Industry plays an important role in the economy, and these countries have substantial productive capacity.

Developed countries also make extensive use of technology. Advanced technological knowledge helps improve production and productivity.

Another characteristic is skilled manpower. Developed economies have workers with advanced technical and professional skills.

Developed countries also have developed transport and communication systems. Good infrastructure supports economic activity.

Developing Countries

A developing country is between these two conditions. Its per-capita income may still be relatively low, but it is rising. Agriculture and industry are gradually developing.

Developing countries can still experience unemployment and economic inequality. Their social environment may also be improving gradually.

Economic growth and economic development are related concepts, but they do not mean exactly the same thing. Economic growth mainly refers to an increase in the quantity of economic output or income.

Economic development is broader and includes both quantitative and qualitative improvements in the economy and people's lives.

Economic growth can be understood as a quantitative increase in total output or national and per-capita income. In simple words, it tells us whether an economy is producing more goods and services than before.

Economic development has a wider meaning. It is the process through which simple, low-income economies transform into modern economies. This transformation involves qualitative as well as quantitative improvements.

The first major difference is meaning. Economic growth means an increase in total output or income. Economic development means a broader transformation that includes income, investment, institutions, technology, human welfare, and quality of life.

The second difference is scope. Economic growth mainly focuses on aggregate production, consumption, and income. Economic development has a wider scope. It includes education, healthcare, infrastructure, quality of life, well-being, equality, inclusion, and sustainability.

Economic growth may occur even when the benefits of increased production are not equally distributed. Therefore, growth does not automatically guarantee that everyone will enjoy a better standard of living.

Economic development has a stronger concern for reducing inequality and improving living standards. It therefore considers the distribution and social effects of economic progress.

Another important difference is human capital. Economic growth does not necessarily have a direct focus on education or health. Economic development deliberately includes education and healthcare as important areas.

Another difference is technology and institutional change. Economic growth can be measured mainly through increased output and income. Economic development includes broader institutional and technological transformation.

Sustainability is another important difference. Economic development aims to balance growth with equity, inclusion, and environmental sustainability.

The simplest way to remember the distinction is that economic growth measures how much an economy produces, while economic development measures how well the economy transforms and improves people's lives.

Economic growth can therefore be considered one part of development. An economy generally needs increased production and income to improve living standards. However, increased production alone is not sufficient for complete development.

Economic development is multi-dimensional. Measures related to development include GNP per capita, population growth, occupational structure of the labour force, urbanization, consumption per capita, infrastructure, literacy rate, life expectancy, healthcare, caloric intake, and infant mortality.

Therefore, economic growth is mainly a quantitative concept, while economic development is both quantitative and qualitative. Growth focuses on increases in output and income. Development focuses on income as well as changes in education, health, infrastructure, human capital, institutions, technology, equality, and sustainability.

In conclusion, economic growth means a quantitative rise in total output or national and per-capita income. Economic development is a broader process involving both quantitative and qualitative improvements.

Capitalistic Economy

A capitalistic economy is an economic system where the factors of production are mainly owned by private individuals and businesses. Private ownership is one of the most important characteristics of this system.

The main objective of producers in a capitalistic economy is profit maximization. Businesses produce goods and services with the intention of earning profit.

Another important characteristic is the role of the market mechanism. Prices are mainly determined by the forces of demand and supply.

Competition is also an important feature. Different private businesses compete with each other to attract consumers.

Consumer choice is another characteristic. Consumers generally have the freedom to choose among different goods and services.

Therefore, the major characteristics of a capitalistic economy are private ownership, profit motive, market allocation, competition, demand and supply-based pricing, and consumer choice.

Socialistic Economy

A socialistic economy is an economic system in which the state or government owns and controls major factors of production.

Government planning is an important characteristic. The state decides what should be produced, how much should be produced, and how resources should be allocated.

Individual economic initiative is more limited compared with a capitalistic economy. Consumer choice may also be more limited because the government has greater control over production and distribution.

Thus, the major characteristics of a socialistic economy are state ownership, government control, central planning, limited individual initiative, and limited consumer choice.

Mixed Economy

A mixed economy combines characteristics of both capitalistic and socialistic economies. In this system, both the private sector and the government participate in economic activities.

Private individuals and businesses can own and operate businesses, while the government also owns or controls certain sectors of the economy.

Market forces play an important role in a mixed economy. Demand and supply can influence prices and production decisions. At the same time, the government regulates economic activities and may intervene when necessary.

According to the uploaded course material, Bangladesh follows a mixed economic system.

In conclusion, the three major economic systems are capitalistic, socialistic, and mixed economies. A capitalistic economy mainly depends on private ownership, profit, competition, and market forces. A socialistic economy emphasizes state ownership, government control, and central planning. A mixed economy combines elements of both systems through private ownership, market activities, government ownership, and regulation.
